\paragraph{Zebra.} A pilot on $5{\times}4$ zebra puzzles (from the earlier, subagent-based version of this study; Section~\ref{sec:llmcalls}) and the main run on $6{\times}6$ zebra puzzles both ended without a single training-solver error. Over \EFourZebraGens evaluated generations the $6{\times}6$ run produced \EFourZebraIndiv fair individuals (\EFourZebraLB at L2, \EFourZebraLC at L3) from \EFourZebraAccepted accepted of \EFourZebraProposals proposed edits, and Haiku solved every one of \EFourZebraEvals evaluations exactly; the pilot (\EFourPilotIndiv individuals, \EFourPilotEvals evaluations) was the same. With no error, fitness is zero everywhere and selection has nothing to act on (Figure~\ref{fig:evo}a). Fresh calls of all three models also solved every initial and final individual (Figure~\ref{fig:evo}b). Fair zebra puzzles of this size are therefore not hard for Haiku, and we stopped the zebra runs.

\paragraph{Sudoku.} On Sudoku the training solver did make errors, so selection had something to act on: over \EFourSudokuGens evaluated generations, \EFourSudokuWithError of \EFourSudokuIndiv individuals had at least one evaluation that was not exact. Every edit Sonnet proposed was applied and accepted (\EFourSudokuAccepted of \EFourSudokuProposals), but the children were not deeper than their parents. Sonnet only ever removed givens (142 removals, no additions or moves); repairing a child to uniqueness then added about five givens back from the solution, and 39 of the 40 children were L1, whereas every initial individual was L3. Best fitness fell from \EFourSudokuBestStart to \EFourSudokuBestEnd as lucky first evaluations were averaged out by re-evaluation, and mean fitness ended where it started (\EFourSudokuMeanStart and \EFourSudokuMeanEnd; Figure~\ref{fig:evo}a). Fresh calls solved \EFourSudokuFinalHaiku\% (Haiku), \EFourSudokuFinalSonnet\% (Sonnet) and \EFourSudokuFinalOpus\% (Opus) of the final population exactly, against \EFourSudokuInitialHaiku\%, \EFourSudokuInitialSonnet\% and \EFourSudokuInitialOpus\% of the initial one (Figure~\ref{fig:evo}b; 8 puzzles each, so these differences are within noise). Seven of the eight final individuals are L1, and every wrong held-out answer had at least 86\% of its cells right. Evolution against Haiku thus found Sudoku that it, and once Opus, gets slightly wrong, but they are easy by the ladder: what it exploited is unreliability in writing out a long and simple solution, not a lack of deductive depth.
